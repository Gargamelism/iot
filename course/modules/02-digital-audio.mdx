## Goal

Understand what a stream of 16-bit numbers at 44.1 kHz can and can't represent, and learn the
fixed-point arithmetic you'll use on a chip without a floating-point unit.

## You'll be able to

- State the sampling theorem and predict where any frequency aliases to.
- Relate bit depth to dynamic range and quantization noise.
- Represent fractions as Q15 integers, then multiply, scale and saturate them correctly.
- Explain why a per-sample `float` multiply is expensive on the ESP32-C3.

## Concepts

- **Sampling.** `fs`, Nyquist (`fs/2`), and **aliasing**: `f` and `fs − f` are
  indistinguishable after sampling. Reconstruction and the DAC's anti-imaging filter.
- **Quantization.** N bits give about 6 dB per bit. Quantization noise and dither (briefly).
- **Integer audio.** `int16_t` samples from −32768 to 32767, and headroom.
- **Fixed point.** In Qm.n formats, Q15 means 1 sign bit and 15 fraction bits (0x7FFF ≈
  0.99997). Multiplying two Q15s gives a Q30 in 32 bits; shift right by 15 to get back.
  Rounding versus truncation.
- **Overflow.** Two's-complement *wraparound* is a loud discontinuity; *saturation* clamps.
- **No FPU.** On the C3, `float` math is a software library call: tens of cycles per
  operation. Floats are fine once per block and not fine per sample.

## Explore (notebook)

`02-digital.ipynb`:

1. **Aliasing table.** For sines at 10, 21, 23, 30 and 40 kHz sampled at 44.1 kHz, predict the
   apparent frequency, then verify with an FFT.
2. **Sweep.** Plot the apparent frequency against the true frequency from 0 to 88.2 kHz (the
   "folding" sawtooth).
3. **Quantization.** Quantize a full-scale sine to N = 4…16 bits. Plot the measured SNR against
   N next to `6.02·N + 1.76`.
4. **Q15 in numpy.** Implement `q15_mul` with `np.int32` and compare it with float over 10⁵
   random pairs. Plot the error histogram for truncation versus rounding.
5. **Wrap vs saturate.** Drive a sine at 1.5× full scale. Write one WAV that wraps (cast to
   int16) and one that clips, and listen to both.

**Aha:** the digital world is periodic in frequency, and integers are periodic in amplitude.
Both bite if you forget it.

## Build

**Host-side C++ this week**, not the board. It builds the arithmetic you'll trust for the rest
of the course.

**Add:**

- **A small fixed-point header** for your project. *Example* (illustrative only):

  ```cpp
  constexpr int Q15_SHIFT = 15;
  constexpr int16_t Q15_ONE = 0x7FFF;
  inline int16_t sat16(int32_t x) { return x > 32767 ? 32767 : x < -32768 ? -32768 : x; }
  inline int16_t q15mul(int16_t a, int16_t b) { return sat16(((int32_t)a * b) >> Q15_SHIFT); }
  ```
- **Self-checks** in a `main()` you compile with `g++` on your computer. *Examples:*

  ```
  q15mul(Q15_ONE, 16384) -> 16383   (≈ 0.5)
  q15mul(-32768, -32768) -> 32767   (saturated, not -32768)
  sat16(40000)           -> 32767
  ```

**Constraints:**

- No floats inside `q15mul` and `sat16`.
- No undefined behavior: watch out for left-shifting negative numbers and for signed overflow.

**Deliverable:** the header plus a test program that prints PASS or FAIL per check.

## Design questions

1. Your output is 16-bit. How much of that range will you reserve for headroom when several
   voices add up, and what does that cost in dB of signal-to-noise?
2. Truncation or rounding after a Q15 multiply? Where does the bias of truncation matter, and
   where could it actually *help* (think: guaranteeing a sum can't exceed a bound)?
3. Should "1.0" be 0x7FFF or 0x8000 in your scheme? What breaks with each?
